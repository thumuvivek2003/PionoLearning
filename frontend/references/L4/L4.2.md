# 4.2.2 — C Major Formula

Now you're going to understand **WHY C Major uses only white keys.**

The major scale formula is:

**W – W – H – W – W – W – H**

Where:

* **W = Whole Step = 2 semitones**
* **H = Half Step = 1 semitone**

Starting from C:

**C → D = W**

**D → E = W**

**E → F = H**

**F → G = W**

**G → A = W**

**A → B = W**

**B → C = H**

So:

**C —W— D —W— E —H— F —W— G —W— A —W— B —H— C**

That's the C Major scale.

### 🎯 Important discovery

Notice something:

**E → F** is naturally a half step.

**B → C** is naturally a half step.

That's why we don't need black keys.

So:

> **C Major = all white keys.**

### 🧠 Your exercise

Don't just memorize "C Major has no sharps/flats."

Understand:

**C → D → E → F → G → A → B → C**

already satisfies:

**W-W-H-W-W-W-H**

### ⏱️ Suggested practice

**5 minutes**

### ✅ Move on when

If I ask:

> "Why does C Major have no black keys?"

You should be able to answer:

> "Because the white-key sequence C-D-E-F-G-A-B-C already follows the Major Scale formula W-W-H-W-W-W-H."

# 4.2.3 — RH C Major

Now we actually play it. 🎹

**RH = Right Hand**

For one-octave C Major:

**C D E F G A B C**

Use:

**1 2 3 1 2 3 4 5**

where:

* 1 = Thumb
* 2 = Index
* 3 = Middle
* 4 = Ring
* 5 = Little finger

So:

**C(1) D(2) E(3) F(1) G(2) A(3) B(4) C(5)**

### Ascending

Play:

**C(1) → D(2) → E(3)**

Then tuck your thumb underneath:

**F(1) → G(2) → A(3) → B(4) → C(5)**

### Descending

Now reverse:

**C(5) → B(4) → A(3) → G(2) → F(1)**

Then:

**E(3) → D(2) → C(1)**

### 🚨 Important

Don't try to play fast.

Your first priority is:

**Correct fingering > speed**

### Practice progression

**Round 1:** Very slowly, one note at a time.

**Round 2:** Ascending continuously.

**Round 3:** Descending continuously.

**Round 4:** Ascending + descending.

### ⏱️ Suggested practice

**5–10 minutes**

### ✅ Goal

Your hand should begin to naturally remember:

> **1-2-3, thumb under, 1-2-3-4-5**

### Ascending

**C(5) → D(4) → E(3) → F(2) → G(1)**

Then cross **3 over**:

**A(3) → B(2) → C(1)**

### Descending

Reverse:

**C(1) → B(2) → A(3) → G(1) → F(2) → E(3) → D(4) → C(5)**

### 🧠 Key idea

Right hand:

**1 2 3 → 1 2 3 4 5**

Left hand:

**5 4 3 2 1 → 3 2 1**

Don't worry if this feels awkward initially.

That's completely normal.

# 4.2.5 — Hands Together

Now comes the important coordination exercise.

🎹 **Both hands play C Major simultaneously.**

At first, don't worry about speed.

### Ascending

Both hands begin on C.

Then:

| Count | RH | LH |
| ----- | -- | -- |
| 1     | C  | C  |
| 2     | D  | D  |
| 3     | E  | E  |
| 4     | F  | F  |
| 5     | G  | G  |
| 6     | A  | A  |
| 7     | B  | B  |
| 8     | C  | C  |

So both hands are playing the **same note names at the same time**, although their finger numbers are different.

### Your first target

Play:

**C-D-E-F-G-A-B-C**

with both hands.

Then:

**C-B-A-G-F-E-D-C**

### 🎯 Don't increase speed yet

Instead ask yourself:

> "Are both hands moving together?"

If one hand gets ahead, **stop and restart slowly.**

### Excellent beginner exercise

Play one note per second:

**C ........ D ........ E ........ F ........ G ........ A ........ B ........ C**

This might feel ridiculously slow.

That's actually good.

You're training coordination.

### ⏱️ Suggested practice

**5–10 minutes**

# 4.2.6 — Slow Scale Practice

Now we're going to turn the scale into a **controlled musical exercise**.

Your goal isn't merely:

> "I can play C Major."

Your goal is:

> **I can play C Major evenly, accurately and without tension.**

### Start VERY slowly

For example:

**C — D — E — F — G — A — B — C**

Keep approximately equal spacing between every note.

Don't play:

**C-D-E---F-G-A-B-C**

because the timing is uneven.

Instead:

**C...D...E...F...G...A...B...C**

### Add a metronome

If you're comfortable with a metronome, start around:

**♩ = 50–60 BPM**

Play one note per beat.

Then:

**C D E F G A B C**

After you can do that comfortably, gradually increase the tempo.

### Watch these 4 things

**1. Accuracy**

Are you hitting the correct keys?

**2. Fingering**

Are you using the planned fingers?

**3. Evenness**

Does every note have roughly equal timing?

**4. Relaxation**

Are your shoulders, wrists and fingers relaxed?

### 🚨 Don't chase speed

A slow, clean scale is much more valuable than a fast, messy scale.

Think:

> **Accuracy → Evenness → Relaxation → Speed**

### ⏱️ Suggested practice

**5–10 minutes**

# 4.2.7 — Random Start Practice ⭐

This is where your understanding starts becoming much stronger.

Most beginners memorize:

> C-D-E-F-G-A-B-C

But if I suddenly say:

> "Start from E."

they become confused.

We want to eliminate that.

---

## Exercise 1 — Start from E

Instead of:

**C-D-E-F-G-A-B-C**

play:

**E-F-G-A-B-C**

Then continue upward if you're working across the next octave.

---

## Exercise 2 — Start from G

Play:

**G-A-B-C-D-E-F-G**

---

## Exercise 3 — Start from A

Play:

**A-B-C-D-E-F-G-A**

---

## Exercise 4 — Start from F

Play:

**F-G-A-B-C-D-E-F**

---

### 🎯 What are you actually learning?

You're learning the **relationships between notes**, not merely memorizing one sequence.

For example:

From **C**:

**C → D → E → F → G → A → B**

From **E**:

**E → F → G → A → B → C → D**

From **G**:

**G → A → B → C → D → E → F**

The notes are the same seven notes, but you're starting from a different point.

---

# 🧠 SUPER IMPORTANT: Don't confuse "starting from" with changing the scale

If I say:

> **Start C Major from E**

I'm **not** asking you to play an E Major scale.

You're still using the notes of **C Major**:

**C D E F G A B**

You're simply beginning at E.

This distinction will become very important later when you learn **scale degrees and modes**.
